## Muscle

Ethan Cole had spent his whole life about one bad week away from the other side of the counter.

Where he grew up there were two careers on offer: the law, or the thing the law was for. He'd
picked the one that paid on Fridays. Bail bonds. A man had bailed him out once, literally, cash on
the table at two in the morning, and then kept him around afterwards and taught him the trade,
and that was as close to a second chance as anyone on his street ever got. Night shifts were no
hardship. Bail doesn't keep office hours. Nobody at work noticed when Ethan stopped needing the
days.

Rose had made him. She was Brujah, she worked for the Baron of Kensington, and every so often she
dropped a word nobody had used in a century. Ethan was pretty sure that meant she was ancient. He wouldn't have known the difference
between the nineteen-twenties and the sixteen-hundreds if you'd paid him; old was old, and she had
stories about all of it.

She'd been good to him, was the thing. She'd wanted him. Not what he'd turned out to be, exactly;
the blood in him was thin, and they both knew it. But she was teaching him anyway. The Traditions.
The players. Who you tipped your hat to if you were crossing east through the Prince's ground, and
who you didn't cross at all. He was the runt, but he could hold his own. He could catch up.

Tonight she stopped on a street in Kensington and pointed.

A two-storey house, boarded windows, a dump. Greek letters still on the front, half of them fallen
off. An old frat house, or what was left of one.

"So," Rose said. "You ready to be my muscle tonight?"

"Sure," Ethan said. "I guess. That's what I do."

The Baron, Evelyn, liked people who played with blood to do it carefully, Rose explained. In a
coordinated way. In a way that did not bring the Prince down on Kensington, or his Sheriff, or
CSIS. There was an operation in the basement of this house that had been told to move and hadn't.
They were here to check it was running safe.

Ethan didn't ask what *check* meant. It was pretty clear.

## Sneakernet

The week's batch had Olly wired.

Whatever Rami had in him this time came on like three espressos and a good idea, all the time, for
days. He'd been taking every shortcut in the city at a jog, talking too much, going down streets he
had no business on. If somebody had tried to stick him up that week, Olly would have high-fived him
on the way past and kept going, and left him standing there with his hand up.

Which was fine, because the Pony Express paid for legs, not judgment.

It turned out Kindred who didn't trust phones still needed to move data, and the fastest way to
move a lot of data across a city is still a man with a bag. Olly had a duffel full of hard drives
over his shoulder and a new address in Kensington, and the address turned out to be a half-condemned
frat house with a light on the ground floor and something chemical rising out of the basement.

Bleach. And under the bleach, something worse.

Down the stairs it was a lab. A real one, or near enough. At the far end, a row of desktop towers
wired together and grinding through something. A centrifuge on a bench that had definitely lived in
a hospital until recently. Pots, beakers, a guy hunched over a keyboard hammering out code. Three or
four people, working hard, not looking up.

One of them was at the centrifuge. Olly knew him.

"Todd?" He was grinning before he'd decided to. "Todd, right?"

Todd looked. Looked again. "Holy shit. What the fuck are you doing here?"

"Bringing you hard drives, I guess."

"I didn't know you were also— It's been a crazy year for me." Todd pulled his lip aside with a thumb
and showed a fang. "Did they ghoul you, or are you a biter?"

"Oh, fuck, that's awesome." Olly grinned wider. "Yeah. They've got me going all around town. Guess we
won't be throwing any parties at this house."

"Not today, that's for sure."

They didn't have anything fresh, Todd said, but there were some interesting blends in the fridge if
Olly wanted to try one. How into weed had he been, back in the day? Because they'd worked out
something like weed, in blood, in a bottle. Bottom shelf, on the left.

It was carbonated. Todd told him not to chug it. Olly chugged it, and went and sat in a chair in the
corner, and for a while he thought about alchemy, about the whole idea of it, of turning one thing
into another thing, of being the vessel where that happened. It all seemed very profound. The
towers hummed. He watched the room the way you watch a movie you've seen before.

## You. Sit Down.

Rose went down the stairs first and came up against the operation like weather.

Ethan had never seen her like this. The easy talk was gone. She walked straight to a woman at the
bench, got in her face, and started taking her apart: *I thought we talked about this. What is the
deal here?*

The woman's name was Jaren, and she didn't back up an inch. "We already moved twice. We're not
moving again. You can tell Evelyn to shove it. We're not causing any problems here."

It went up from there. Somebody else, Todd, got to his feet with his hands out, trying to calm it
down. Then Jaren put both hands on Rose and shoved her.

Ethan didn't think about it. He pointed at the one who'd stood up. "You. Sit down."

He said it the way he said things to clients who were about to make his night longer, and Todd sat.
Meekly. Pulled his chair back in like a kid in class.

Ethan went for Jaren. He didn't swing. He just put his shoulder into her and kept walking, and she
went backwards until there was nowhere left to go. Then he stood there and looked big. He was good
at looking big.

Rose straightened her coat. "Unplug those," she said, pointing at the computers. "We're taking
whatever they are somewhere secure. This operation is closed."

Ethan went for the towers. Not the monitors, which were junk. The boxes with the brains in them.
Four desktops and a duffel bag full of hard drives somebody had dropped by the door.

Behind him, Rose was doing something to the room. Ethan felt the edge of it, a pressure, like
standing near a speaker with the bass turned all the way up and no sound coming out. The thin-bloods
felt the whole thing. They sat where they were and watched their work get carried up the stairs.
Nobody tried anything.

In the corner, Olly watched too, from inside the weed-blood, and started to feel like this was his
problem.

These were his people, sort of. Todd was his friend, sort of. The delivery he'd just made was going
up the stairs in the arms of a guy he didn't know. And there was a pistol in his backpack, and the
backpack was on his lap, and he got his hand inside it very slowly, so nobody would notice. The gun
seemed to keep moving away from his fingers. The whole room had gone paranoid.

Todd had a single tear of blood running down one cheek. He wasn't wiping it off.

Olly stood up. He walked over to Todd with the gun in his hand, down at his side, because he had
forgotten it was there.

"Todd," he whispered. "What's going on? Who are these people? Why are they taking the servers?"

Rose turned and leaned on him, the same way she was leaning on everyone else. He felt it. He felt the
room wanting him to look at her, and be quiet, and sit. But whatever Rami had cooking in him this
week was louder than Rose. He stayed on Todd.

"I don't know, man." Todd was stammering. "Evelyn keeps shutting us down. We're trying to follow the
rules. I don't know what the deal is."

## Fire

Ethan was coming back down the stairs for the lab equipment when he saw it all at once.

The guy with the gun, talking to Todd. Rose, turned toward them, distracted. Jaren, edging closer to
Todd. And the third one, the one nobody had been watching, tipping back a metal flask and drinking the
whole thing down, walking up behind Rose while he did it.

Ethan moved before he had a plan. He put himself between the flask and Rose's back, which was the
whole plan. Be the wall. Find out what's happening. React.

The guy spat.

It came out of him as fire. There was no lighter, no spark, nothing to catch. He just breathed out
and the breath was burning, and it hit Ethan square in the face.

Something at the very bottom of Ethan started screaming. The oldest thing in him. The thing that
knows what fire does to the dead. He heard it, and he muted it, and he kept his feet. He was on fire
and he knew it and he dropped anyway, because that's what you do: stop, drop, roll. He heard Rose
grabbing for something to beat him out with.

Olly didn't have that kind of control.

The fire went up and so did every thought in his head. There was only the door. The door was up the
stairs, and the stairs were past Rose and past the man rolling on the floor, and Olly went over him,
literally over him, and up and out into the street and kept going, and did not once look back to see
if anyone was behind him.

---

When Ethan could think again, he was lying on the basement floor, smoking, and it was very quiet.

He hurt. He'd started to get used to how little things hurt now, bruises and cuts that just closed.
This hurt worse than he remembered burns hurting when he was alive. He lay there and let the scream at
the bottom of him settle, and then he looked around.

Rose was standing. Rose was fine.

Nobody else in the basement was alive. Three of them were still there, on the floor or slumped at the
benches, not burned, not torn, not marked in any way he could see. Just stopped. Their faces had
frozen in the middle of something, and the something was horror.

The one who'd breathed fire wasn't among them.

"Can you walk?" Rose said. "Do you need help getting out?"

"I can walk." He got up. "But that fucking hurt."

"Fire's nothing to mess around with." She was looking at the bodies, and then at the empty flask on
the floor, with a different kind of interest. "I'm very intrigued by that flask. We'll take everything.
All of it." She turned back to him. "Did you see who ran out?"

"Honestly? I barely noticed him."

"Then that's your job, once you've rested. A loose thread. Find him."

## Sandman

Ralph Begg was handsome, and he'd been handsome his whole life, and it had gotten him about as far as
handsome gets you.

He'd been a jock in high school who couldn't quite make it at the next level. After that, a string of
dot-com jobs he was good at right up until someone noticed what else he was doing with the access.
Then a con. Then a job. Then a con. When California stopped being any fun he came home to Toronto and
moved into the basement of an old high-school friend's house in the Junction Triangle. The friend was
one of the few people Ralph had never taken advantage of. He'd decided to keep it that way.

Then he found a way to skim paychecks out of a gig-economy app, and the app turned out to belong to
something that was not a person.

He never saw it coming. He only saw it at the end: bald, big ears, too many teeth, like Count Orlok
out of the old silent movie. It had picked him for a reason, he gathered later, a pretty boy with
computer skills, something it could use and make ugly. Except Ralph didn't get ugly. Whatever it gave
him didn't take right. It got angry about that, and it broke his neck and left him in the gutter.

He woke up the next night with his head on backwards.

It turned around, eventually. Most things did. Then he spent three weeks working out what he was, the
way he'd worked out everything else in his life: alone, by trial and error, with a laptop. The sun hurt.
Food came back up. People didn't seem to mind very much if you drank from them, as long as you didn't
wake them. And somebody, some time in the last ten years, had very carefully scrubbed every word about
creatures like him off the internet. The holes were still there if you knew how to look for holes.
Ralph knew how to look for holes. That was when he got scared.

He fed like a sandman. Middle-class streets, quiet houses, back doors that stuck but opened. A nice
athleisure set so he looked like a guy out for a jog at three in the morning, which people will
accept. In and out. Nobody woke up.

He was coming home from one of those nights, cutting through an alley, when he saw a couple kissing up
against a fence.

It was only the angle. The streetlight caught their silhouette exactly right. And then it wasn't a kiss
anymore. It was feeding, and it was vicious, and for the first time in his life he was seeing it done by
someone who wasn't him.

He should have kept walking. He didn't. He went quiet instead, a trick he'd found he could do, making
himself into a gap in the night where no sound came from. He went down the far side of the alley,
through the shadows, until he was fifteen feet away.

Two men. One tall and thin, six-four at least, in an old-fashioned suit. In his right hand, the hand
nearest Ralph, he held something long and pale. The other one was shorter, younger, in a stage magician's
costume, and he was pinned against the fence and struggling, and the struggling was getting weaker.

Ralph didn't feed until the kill. He'd never let it get close.

He let the quiet go and kicked over a garbage can.

The tall one swung around, and he did it without letting go, like a dancer dipping his partner, his
mouth still locked on the other man's throat, and his eyes found Ralph's.

The victim's mouth was open, and there were fangs in it. It was one of them, feeding on another one of
them. And the thing in the tall man's hand caught the light, and Ralph saw it was a bone, a long one, a
femur, and it was carved all the way up and down.

"Sorry," Ralph said. "Didn't mean to interrupt. I was just going through the alley here. Sorry."

Something came into his head then, from outside, and pushed.

---

Some time later, he found himself walking. Different street. Same neighbourhood. The sky had changed
colour in the east.

He should have run for home. It wasn't far. He went back to the alley instead, because he had been
careful for three weeks and he was done with careful. He had to know.

Blood on the gravel. A trail of it, going nowhere he could follow. No body. No tall man. But behind the
overturned can, where it had been thrown and forgotten, the femur.

He picked it up. It looked like something out of a museum, the kind of thing a colonizer stole from
somewhere a hundred years ago. The writing on it wasn't anything he could read.

Then, because nobody was watching and he was who he was, he touched two fingers to the blood on the
gravel and tasted it.

It wasn't like the blood he knew. If that was savoury, this was something else: deeper, stranger. Umami.
It lit up something in him that nothing had lit before.

He put the bone inside his Patagonia vest and jogged home.

His roommate was up, finishing a beer at the kitchen table. "Meet any girls out jogging?"

"Tough night," Ralph said. "Just here for the cardio."

He went down to the room at the back of the basement, the one with no windows, and locked the door,
and slept like the dead, which was what he was.

## Methuselah's Ossuary

He didn't carry the bone at first. You don't walk around Toronto with a human femur. He put it in the
safe and took pictures instead, high resolution, every inch of it. He printed himself a student card for
York University, pulled on a hoodie, and tailgated a real student through the
library door.

Anthropology. Ancient scripts. Then more ancient scripts.

The writing, he worked out, was cuneiform. Or something descended from it: the same wedges and marks,
not as old as the Babylonian tablets in the books, but uncanny how close. Some of the characters verged
on drawings. That gave him a second question, and the second question took him further. Over the
nights that followed he stopped doing anything else.

He couldn't do it alone, so he didn't. He wrote to experts on the other side of the world, a professor
of ancient languages in Mumbai, nobody local, from accounts he'd built for the purpose, and he read
everything they sent back.

A phrase kept turning up. *Methuselah's ossuary.* Bones like his bone. Treated as deeply magical,
turning up in charnel houses and old collections all over the world. None of them as old as Babylon,
whatever the dealers claimed. He found references to four or five. One in Syria. One in Greece. One in
Chile. One in Singapore. And one that was plainly a fake, carved in a language somebody had made up
after 1950.

By the third night he'd stopped leaving the bone in the safe. He carried it everywhere.

Then two messages came in, to an account nobody should have been able to find.

*I have an interested buyer if you're selling.*

He blocked that one.

*Do you want to know what it says?*

He answered that one.

They wrote back the same night. *Where would you like to meet?*

He'd never said where he was. They knew anyway, and closely. That terrified him. It also made the decision
for him: he had to learn. And he was a vampire now, wasn't he? He must be able to take care of himself.
He picked somewhere public: the Commons, the park by York. He put a fat envelope of cash in his pocket,
thought about a gun, and decided against it.

He wore a salmon-coloured polo shirt so they'd know him.

## A Long-Lost Cousin

Ethan's burns had healed, mostly. He was hungry, but he was all right. Rose had come to get him with
another job.

"I'm not sure about the person we're meeting," she said, as they crossed the grass toward the lights.
"They have something much more valuable than they know. I'm trying to work out how stupid they are, or
how corrupt."

"Bring me along in case they're very stupid or very corrupt?"

"That's our guy." She pointed at the salmon polo. "Go and introduce yourself. Get a read on him. Come
back when you're done."

Ethan went over. The guy was standing with his hands in his pockets, radiating confidence at nobody.

"My boss sent me," Ethan said. "You have the bone?"

"Not on me. We're here to talk about it, not hand anything over." Friendly. Easy. "It's in a safe place."

"Fair enough."

"We're strangers. Let's not be. I'm Ralph."

"Ethan."

"Nice to meet you, Ethan."

So how had Ralph found it? A blind auction, Ralph said. A storage unit, packed in with some less
impressive antiques. No, he didn't know who'd owned it; with blind storage auctions they legally can't
tell you. He did it every now and then. You could flip some money if you were careful: watches, bonds,
old guns. Though ever since *Storage Wars* there were too many buyers and the margins weren't what they
used to be.

It was a good story. It was a very smooth story. Ethan couldn't tell Ralph wasn't human; Ralph looked
warm, and breathed, and his heart was going like anybody's.

He walked back to Rose. "Well, he's not stupid. I don't know about corrupt. Not saying he is. Just
don't think he's dumb enough to try anything."

Rose went over herself.

"I hear your name's Ralph. I'm Rose." She smiled. "Quite the find you have. What are your hopes for it?"

Ralph said he had higher-resolution photographs than the ones he'd shared, if she'd like to see them.
She'd implied she could tell him what it was.

"If it's real, and not a replica, it's a ritual object. For the dead, or for doing things to the dead.
That's typical of things like this." She opened a laptop that folded back into a tablet, and flicked
through images of the bone that someone had marked up with a digital pen. Repeating shapes, circled.
"I'm not fluent. But this is a word for *horse*. This one is *lift*, or *threshold*. I don't have it all
yet, and it isn't a one-person job. The people I'm working with want to know it's genuine. And they want
to know it's safe."

Ralph did his best. He wasn't selling anything. He'd confirmed it was real human bone, that the carving
was as old as the bone, not added later. He could share the scans. If they could work together to find
out where it came from, he'd even let her handle it, somewhere controlled. But what was *her* interest?

The pressure came then, and it was not like sleep, though it was the closest thing he had to compare it
to. It was his words being nudged, gently and firmly, in a direction that wasn't his.

Rose looked at him strangely. Then she reached out, took his chin in her hand, and lifted his lip with
her thumb.

"Who's your sire?"

"My—father?"

"Who made you like this?"

He felt himself answer. "He looked like Count Orlok. Have you ever seen *Nosferatu*? Bald, big ears,
too many teeth."

Rose turned to Ethan, delighted. "I think we've found ourselves a long-lost cousin."

Ethan stood up straighter. This wasn't some human he could put in the ground if things went wrong.
This was one of them, and he had no idea what it could do.

Rose went on with the questions. Who did Ralph work with? *Himself.* Did he know who his Prince was?
Ralph had never heard the word used like that. She rattled off names, the Prince, Baron Evelyn, a few
others, and none of them meant anything to him. She laughed out loud.

"Well, isn't it our great fortune that we found you before you were torn to shreds." She let go of his
chin. "We need to get you, and that bone, somewhere safe before somebody comes hunting. Do you have a
secure place?"

He did, he said. But there was a roommate, and the roommate was in the dark, and Ralph was keeping him
there. He'd worked out on his own that you don't tell anyone. The feds were looking for anybody who said
the word.

Rose looked impressed, and Ralph understood she thought he was keeping his food close to home. He didn't
correct her.

Ethan had somewhere. His office. It got some foot traffic in the day, but it had bars on the doors,
because now and then a client needed holding. It wasn't much. But Ralph could stay there, for now.

"I'll leave the door unlocked," Ethan said. "Which will be a first for me."

And the bone? Rose asked. Would Ralph like it stored somewhere else? Somewhere she knew was secure?

"The bone is safe," Ralph said. "I can get to it when I need it."

Not the answer she wanted. She let it go. She tilted her head at Ethan, and they stepped outside.

"If he goes anywhere," Rose said quietly, "follow him. If he spends the night here, go and search his
house."

## A Minor Boon

Olly went to Rami, after, because there wasn't anyone else to go to.

He told him all of it. The frat house, the drop-off. How there'd seemed to be a chain of command that
nobody had told him about, and his delivery had gone up the stairs in some stranger's arms. Whether any
of that was *allowed*. And the guy with the flask. The fire.

Rami listened soberly. The fire interested him; Olly could see it turning over behind his eyes. But he
set it aside.

"It is not ideal," he said. "There are processes we can pull on. But I have something more urgent for
you. I will owe you a minor boon for it." He folded his hands. "A friend of mine. Do you know what
diablerie is?"

Olly knew. It was when one of them drank another one dry, all the way down, and came out stronger.

"I dealt with the body," Rami said. "Claude and I have a space in the coroner's office where it is
hidden, for now. We have been looking for anything that points to who did it. Anything you find would
be very valuable to me. This is the kind of thing that brings the hammer down on all of us."

"You want me on a murder mystery?" Olly said. "I'm not an investigator. I bring things places. I scope
out abandoned buildings. You put drugs in my body."

"I will be blunt with you." Rami gestured at the lab around them, the apparatus bubbling, the careful
pressures. "If I could trust you with this equipment, I would leave you here to watch it and go myself.
There is no one I trust with it. Weeks of work, months. If it boils wrong, if the pressure goes wrong, I
start again from nothing, and I do not have the ingredients to start again. So I am here, and you are
who I have. You are not my first choice. You are not my second." He looked at Olly. "But it is my friend.
I do not want to do nothing. And if I wait until I am free, the trail goes cold."

Olly thought about it.

"Well," he said. "Naturally I'm forever in your debt for sinking your fangs into me."

He meant it. There wasn't any irony in it at all.

"So yes. I'll go."
