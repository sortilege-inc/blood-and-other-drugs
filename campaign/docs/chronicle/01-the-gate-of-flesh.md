## St. Michael's

The clinic closed at ten. That was the rule, and Claude kept it the way she kept all the rules
that were written down, which was mostly.

She was stacking intake forms in the back of the van when he came up the sidewalk. Good coat.
Good shoes. Not a man who needed a street clinic, and not a man who looked like he'd come to
volunteer, so she waited for him to say what he wanted.

He wanted to talk about her research.

He knew about it, which should have bothered her more than it did: how to treat people who never
came back to the same clinic twice, who were somewhere else by the time the results came in. He'd
read it. He had questions, and they were good
questions, and somewhere around the third one she realized she'd stopped stacking forms.

Then he asked her what happened to research like hers when the researcher died.

She knew the answer. Everyone in the field knew it. Someone gets on a roll, a real one, the kind
of streak you get once in a career, and then they get sick or they get hit by a bus, and the
whole thing stops. Their notes sit in a box. Somebody picks it up three years later and spends
two of them working out what the first person already knew in their sleep. Momentum doesn't
transfer. You can't bequeath it.

"Unless," he said, "you don't die."

His name was Dominik Levesque. He talked for a long time about legacy, and discovery, and what a
mind like hers could do if it simply kept going, fed the right resources, for as long as it
wanted. It was a sales pitch. She knew it was a sales pitch. She was thirty years old and very good
at her job, and she said yes.

---

She woke in the dark.

Not a room. Something tighter than a room, with walls that didn't give when she hit them, and she
hit them for a long time. Hours. Maybe longer. Maybe more than one night; there was no way to
tell, and no clock in her body anymore to tell it by. There was only the hunger, which was not
like being hungry. It was like being a hole.

When the lid finally came off, it wasn't to let her out.

He had brought something. She never learned what. It might not have been a person; she has
thought about that more than she would like, and she still doesn't know. She fed on it until the
screaming in her head went quiet enough to hear him, and what she heard was disgust.

She was the wrong thing. She understood that much before she understood anything else. He'd
wanted a successor, someone to carry his work in the Blood forward, and what he'd made was thin.
Weak. A mistake with a pulse she could fake.

Toronto has rules about mistakes like her. Other cities are kinder; this one isn't. Nobody who
holds a seat at the Prince's court may be thin-blooded, or may have made one. And a thin-blood
who meets the Final Death has not, in the eyes of the court, been murdered. Killing her would
cost him nothing. Keeping her would cost him everything.

He couldn't do it anyway.

Whatever it was, cowardice or something that wanted to be mercy, he couldn't put her down
himself. So he walked her across the city with his hand on her arm, and he gave her to someone
called Moth. They spoke for a minute, too low for her to follow. Something changed hands.

She found out much later what it was, and what it had been meant to buy.

She hasn't seen Dominik Levesque in five years.

## The Basement

Olly found the building on his own, which was the whole point.

Most of what he did ran on tips. Somebody knew somebody who'd been in the old bottling plant, or
the tunnel under the rail yards, and the spots moved through the scene like currency, and by the
time you got there, three other crews had already tagged the walls. This one was his. Nobody had
posted it. Nobody had talked about it at any party he'd been to. He knew the signs of an empty
building the way other people knew their own street, and this one had all of them.

He went down to the basement with his camera and the flash up. Pop. The dark jumped back and
settled again. Pop. That one came out good. He could already see the flyer.

Then he stepped through a doorway and the place wasn't empty anymore.

No cookware, which ruled out meth. No bed, which ruled out squatters. A table with papers on it.
Notebooks, stacks of them, every page crammed edge to edge with tight little cursive, and
symbols in the margins: triangles with circles in them, planets maybe, like some astrology girl
lived here. The writing was mostly English and then it wasn't, sliding into
other alphabets and back again mid-sentence. He flipped to the back. The last entry was a night
old. Two at most.

Okay. Occupied. Not his.

He turned to go and there was a man standing right behind him, close enough to touch, and the man
opened his mouth and Olly saw the teeth.

---

He woke up tilted.

Strapped at the wrists and ankles to a board, the kind paramedics carry people down stairs on,
bolted to a heavy wooden table that had been hinged so it could pivot. He was hanging at about
forty-five degrees. His mouth tasted like pennies. And he was hungry in a way he had no word for.

Olly had done a lot of drugs. He had never done this one.

He started screaming. He screamed questions, and when the questions didn't work, he screamed
everything else. The man at the other table kept working. Eventually he set down whatever he was
holding, walked over, and slapped Olly across the face.

Olly kept screaming. The man slapped him again. Not hard. Not angry. The way you'd tap a vending
machine. He kept doing it, patiently, until Olly understood that this was going to go on for
exactly as long as the screaming did, and stopped.

"Usually," the man said, "the penalty for interrupting my space is death." The accent was thick,
Egyptian. The voice was very calm. "But you could be very useful. Would you like to be useful, or
would you like to die?"

"There's no law against taking pictures," Olly said. "I was just taking pictures. Let me go.
You'll never see me again."

"You fail to understand." The man smiled, and there were the teeth again. "I have drained you of
your blood. I have made you into one of my own kind. You will need to drink blood as well."

He explained some things. Olly didn't take in most of them. Then the man pressed his own wrist to
Olly's mouth, and for a second everything was very, very good, and then the wrist was gone and the
hole in Olly was only a little smaller than before.

The man's name was Rami. He had two conditions.

The first was that he would feed on Olly. Carefully. Sustainably. That was the word he used.

The second took longer to explain. Rami would put things into him, precursors, he called them, and
Olly's body would do something to them over a week or two, and then Rami would take them out
again. A furnace, Rami said. A vessel for the work. And as long as Olly came when he was called and
did not interfere with the work, he was free to be his own man.

What got to Olly, in the end, wasn't the vampire part. Vampires being real was fine. Vampires being
real was honestly kind of cool. What got to him was the rudeness of it. This was a guy who hung out
underground. Olly hung out underground. They should have been chill. He said so, more than once,
while Rami talked right through him.

But Rami never raised his voice. Never hurried. Somewhere in all that calm, Olly's heart stopped
pounding, and it was a while before he noticed he didn't have one to pound. He chose to be useful.
Rami undid the straps.

## The Furnace

The name *Hollis* turned up in the first few months: in the margins of the notebooks, in things Rami
said and then stopped saying. Whoever Hollis was, he'd been here before Olly.

Olly learned the shape of it. Rami didn't deal in volume. He made one thing, more or less, the
Kindred answer to ecstasy, and he made it for customers he plainly despised. He never touched it
himself. To make it, he needed what he called an *athanor corporis*: the furnace of the body. A
living vessel to cook in.

"I want to cook something up," Rami would say, or "I need to boil something," and that meant an IV.
Three or four times a month, never more than an hour or two. Sometimes a bag went in and came out a
week later and a new one went in. Sometimes he got a week and a half off.

Olly was never high, exactly. His body was doing the work, not enjoying it. But the work had
weather. One batch left him wired for a week, ceiling-staring, talking a mile a minute to anyone
who'd stand still. Another dropped him into a hole so deep he couldn't find the edges. Rami taught
him about resonance, how blood carries the mood of whoever it came out of, and started sending
him out with instructions. *Find me sanguine blood this week. Something hot.* Olly got good at
reading people. He got good at feeding against the swing, a downer for the up weeks, an upper for
the down, like a guy managing his own meds out of other people's arms.

Sometimes Rami fed on him, and that was strange too. Olly knew what it was like for the people he
fed on: not agony, not even close, more like a slow blissed-out drift. It turned out it was like
that for him too. When Rami fed, a warm flat calm came over him, the feeling of lying down in a
real bed after three days awake. *This is fine. This is okay.* It lingered for a while afterward.

He didn't think much about the arrangement. That was the idea.

---

He still threw parties. Of course he did.

Olly's life before hadn't stopped because he'd died. The scene didn't know, and he wasn't going to
tell it. He found spots. He talked himself up the sides of buildings he had no business climbing,
because he'd said out loud that he would, and saying it out loud was how he made himself do
things. Once, in a tunnel with a curved arch and acoustics like a cathedral, he'd thrown the
biggest one he'd ever thrown: speakers stacked at one end, drum and bass loud enough to feel in
your fillings, and the word let all the way out, normies and all, because a spot like that you
only ever used once.

Now the parties were how he ate.

Two people helped. Dmitri was urbex, a mortal who knew where Olly's signal spots were and texted
when he had something. What Dmitri had was usually a crypto guy. Some kid who'd made it big on a
coin with a dog on it and wanted the real thing now, the private tour, no posers, the best view
in the city. Dmitri sold them the view. Olly met them at the top.

Olly had rules about it, mostly. He went for people who had it coming, or near enough: the frat
boys, the Bitcoin bros, the guys who wandered into the scene like it owed them something. People
who needed taking down a notch.

The other one was Trieste.

He'd met her at a rave. She could walk into any bar in the city and walk out with the worst man in
it, and she'd bring him somewhere abandoned, and there Olly would be. She had a crew she ran with,
half a dozen of them. She'd asked Olly along more than once, and he'd always found a reason not to
go.

One night they'd holed up together in one of his spots. She stretched when the sun went down and
said she had something to show him, and there on a mirror were two lines of cocaine.

Black.

She did one and handed him the bill. He had it halfway to his nose before he stopped. "What *is*
this?"

She looked at him like he'd asked something weird. "This was Jonah."

Jonah. He knew the name. One of the crew.

"What did you do to Jonah?"

"I didn't *do* anything." She shrugged. "It was sunrise. This is what we do with them after. I cut
some L into it." L was Rami's. Olly knew that much. L was the thing Olly was the furnace for. "It's
a good way to remember someone."

He did the line.

It was the best he'd felt since he died.

Later, she told him about it. Once a month, she and her crew went somewhere with a view of the
east and sat out and watched the sun come up, and waited to see who didn't quite make it back
inside in time. She knew somebody who could brew something that helped with the burns, if he was
nervous.

"You guys are into some weird shit," Olly said.

"It's a bonding activity."

"Jonah," he said. "I won't forget him."

"See?" said Trieste. "It works."

He told her he'd come. Not next month. He had an event coming up. But after that, sure, for real.
He'd said it out loud now. That was how he made himself do things.

## Stash Number Three

Moth never stayed anywhere longer than seven nights.

It was the curse of Moth's line, Moth explained, the way someone else might explain a bad knee.
Stay put past the seventh night and the Blood turns on you. So Moth kept a loop: storage units,
warehouses, crates of old museum pieces, and a dozen little troves in between, each stocked with
whatever Moth had gathered. Books. Bones. Things in jars. Moth was a magpie with a
library card. And every few nights they were on the move again, and sometimes the move went
sideways: *Not that one. We'll skip that one for now. Actually, I need the thing in stash number
three.*

Moth looked about thirty and was probably ten years older than that. Claude and Moth were not
friends, exactly. Claude respected Moth, and she
wasn't sure yet that she trusted Moth, and Moth seemed to find both of those things correct. It
was closer to fostering: every new safehouse came with a new book, and every book came with a quiz.

The book Moth kept coming back to was a bad one.

*Le Livre Antique du Sang*: the Ancient Book of Blood, rendered from Persian into French by
Péladan, the occultist, who had apparently not been much of a translator. Claude's French wasn't
much better. Between the two of them they worked at it for years, and it gave up its meanings
slowly. It described a process. A series of transformations the author called *gates*.

Moth read. Claude tried things.

That was the difference, and it was why it worked. Moth collected; Moth didn't *do*. Claude was a
researcher, and a researcher who reads a procedure wants to run it. Her first attempts failed, badly
and instructively. She learned what equipment she needed, and what she was missing, and Moth
started buying ingredients alongside the artifacts. Through Moth's dealers, and their dealers,
Claude caught the edges of something she hadn't known was there: a scattered, unofficial community
across Toronto of people doing strange things with blood. No meetups. No names. Just a referral
here, a supplier's other buyer mentioned there.

One night Moth set out everything for the next chapter before Claude had even reached it. There was
one jar Claude didn't recognize.

"The ashes of a vampire," Moth said. "That one was hard to find."

"What did you trade for it?"

"It's fine. Someone owed me a boon. I forgave it."

Claude knew enough by then to know what a boon was: a favour, owed by something that would not
die, that could be called in a century later. Currency, of a kind nobody printed. She thought about
asking where the ashes had come from.

She didn't. She was too excited to run the experiment.

---

The next safehouse in the loop had been torn apart.

Not robbed. Searched. Drawers out, boxes slit, the kind of mess people make when they're looking for
one specific thing and don't find it. They didn't stay. They went on to the next spot on the list,
and went in together, circling the room in opposite directions. Nothing out of place. Moth set a
little alarm at the door, just in case, and they settled in.

That was the night Moth told her the truth.

Over the past months Moth had been explaining where things came from, who sold what and for how
much, because Claude would need to know eventually. And so that night Moth told her about the book.
About how it had come to Moth: payment, from a Tremere named Dominik Levesque, for a job. The job
was her.

Moth hadn't done it, obviously. And Moth was fairly sure Dominik had thought the book was worthless
when he handed it over.

Dominik was close with the Prince, Moth said. His library was supposed to be extraordinary. Good
luck ever seeing it.

Then Moth told her something more useful. The process in the book that had been stalling her for
weeks: Moth was fairly sure, now, what it was describing. And there was a supplier who
had the equipment for it, and who might lend it. His name was Rami.

## Daylight

The alarm didn't go off.

Claude woke anyway, and the wrongness of it hit her before anything else. The sun was still up. She
could feel it through the earth, a weight on her body like wet sand. And there were people at the
door. More than one.

She crawled to Moth. She shook Moth. She said Moth's name, then shouted it. Nothing. Moth was down
in the day the way the dead are down.

The door came in.

Three of them, maybe four. Tactical gear, bug-eyed helmets, rifles. Lights so bright they turned the
room white. Claude went behind whatever was nearest and made herself small. She was not going to
leave Moth.

One of them walked over to Moth where Moth lay.

*Pop. Pop.*

Claude didn't decide anything after that.

There was a strength in her that she had never felt and didn't know she had. She came out of hiding
and took the first one's arm off at the shoulder. It came away clean. The second one she caught by
the throat, and then she didn't have a throat in her hand so much as the inside of one. The third
got his rifle around toward her and she took it out of his hands and drove the stock back into his
face.

She was on top of one of them and drinking before she knew it. She had never killed anyone. She'd
built a whole way of living around making sure she never would. The thing that had come out of hiding
didn't care about any of that.

She stopped anyway. She doesn't know how. She pulled her mouth off him while there was still
something left in him.

Then she went to Moth.

One of them was dead, and not from feeding. Two were down and bleeding. One had run. She dug the
bullets out of Moth with whatever she had, and then she looked at the two who were still breathing
and thought about consent, and how the question had stopped applying the moment they came through
the door. She drained one of them nearly dry, into Moth, so that Moth could lie still and heal and
not have to hunt for it.

The other she left where he lay. They took what they could carry and went somewhere new, somewhere
that wasn't on the loop at all, and they didn't go back.

## The Curse

Something went wrong with Rami.

It started like any feeding. The warm, flat calm; the bed after three days awake. Olly was halfway
asleep in it when he noticed, the way you notice an alarm going off in someone else's apartment. It
had gone on longer than it usually did.

"Hey," he said. "Rami."

Rami grunted. Heard him. Didn't stop.

It came on like swimming too deep: the slow realization that the surface is further than you
thought, then the fast one, that you may not make it. Olly started to struggle. Rami held on. They
went off the chair together, locked up, stumbling across the lab in a clinch with Rami's mouth still
at his neck and Olly's strength going out of him in pulls.

His bag was hanging on the corner of a shelf.

He'd always carried. Nobody had ever asked him why, which was good, because the answer was mostly
*because*. He dragged the two of them over to the bag. He got a hand in. He got a hand on the
holster, and then on the grip, and he put three rounds into Rami's stomach at a distance of no
inches at all.

Rami let go.

He reeled back and dropped into the chair Olly had been sitting in and stayed there, holding
himself. He didn't get up. He didn't come at Olly again.

Olly paced. He had trusted this guy. That was what his brain kept circling back to, while his hands
went looking for something to tie with, because tying Rami down seemed like the kind of thing a
person should do in this situation, even if Olly wasn't sure it would help. He found straps. He got
Rami's wrists fixed to the chair.

Then he stepped back and watched Rami heal.

Rami breathed deep and slow, like someone concentrating very hard, and the holes in him closed. It
was not a fast process, but it was one Olly could see happening, flesh drawing back in on itself
like film run backwards. When it was done, Rami's eyes opened, and he sat forward against the straps.

"The curse got me," he said. "I am sorry for having endangered you."

"What do you mean? Were you *planning* that?"

"No. You are of such use to me. I would never endanger you willingly."

Olly stared at him. "Is this what happened to the last guy?"

"No." Something crossed Rami's face. "Hollis was a fool. He insulted the Prince." He let his head
fall back. "I am Banu Haqim. My sire's sire's sire, back and back — it is our curse. When we feed
upon the blood of our own kind, sometimes we cannot stop." He looked at Olly. "This may be your
curse as well. But unlike me, you do not need the blood of our kind to live."

"It's real convenient that you feed off me, then." Olly's voice came out high. "Look. It's been
good. This has been going great. But I don't want that to happen again."

Rami didn't answer. He only let his eyes close, as if to say *another night*.

Olly undid the straps. "Shooting you didn't seem to do too much," he said. "You need anything? Are
you going to be okay?"

Rami had fed enough, he said. More than enough. He leaned back against the stretcher-board and
didn't move.

Olly got his bag. On the way out, without turning around, he mumbled, "Sorry I shot you."

## The Gate of Flesh

A few nights later, Moth took Claude to meet Rami.

He'd gotten Moth's messages. He had exactly what she needed. And it was good timing, he said; he
would send her his boy. Olly would come and help with her project, and when she was finished, Moth
could consider the favour between them paid, and Olly could come home. He pressed his thumb into a
small token and gave it to her. Olly would know what it meant.

She went alone.

Olly was sitting outside one of the houses he crashed at, smoking. He didn't get up.

"I think we're supposed to meet?" Claude said.

"There's no party tonight."

"No, no. Rami sent me. You're supposed to help me."

He took the token and looked at it and then looked at her properly for the first time. "You saw
Rami? How's he doing?"

"He seemed all right. Kind of a weird guy."

"Yeah." Olly laughed once, not really a laugh. "What's your name?"

"Claudette. But you can call me Claude."

"Okay. I'm Olly. What am I helping you with?"

She glanced at the house. "Are there people around?"

"In the house. They're cool." He considered her. "Maybe let's go for a walk."

They walked. She talked, and it was too much and too fast and pitched too high for someone who'd
never read a page of it, and she knew it and couldn't stop. The book. The process. How the book said
she needed an athanor to make it do what it promised. How apparently he was one.

"Yeah," Olly said. "Rami uses me as some kind of drug vial for whatever he's cooking."

The book called the process a series of gates, she told him. And the first gate was the Gate of Flesh.

"I'm the Gate of Flesh."

"Yes."

"Jesus." He smoked. "Okay."

"If that's all right."

"I never get a say in this."

"No, it's really important that you get a say." She stopped walking. "Rami gave you to Moth, my
teacher, as payment for a favour. I know how that sounds. But if it hurts, or anything, we don't have
to do it again. Just once. I just need to see if it works."

"And what are you getting out of this?"

"Knowledge."

"What am I getting out of this?"

She didn't have an answer ready, so she asked him instead. "What do you want?"

He thought about it for a while. "What's the next gate?"

"The Gate of Names."

Olly flicked the end of his cigarette into the street. "When you get to the Gate of Names," he said,
"can I come too?"

"Yeah," said Claude. "Yeah."
