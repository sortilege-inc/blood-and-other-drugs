## Rounds

Claude still worked hospitals.

Not the way she used to. She followed Moth's loop around the city, and wherever the safehouse was that week, she
found the nearest hospital and made herself useful at night. Some of it was real medicine still. Some of it was
asking, while she had the needle in anyway, whether she might take a little extra. And some of it was research. She
looked for people with blood disorders, sickle cells and strange counts and things carried in the blood, and she
ran what she took against her own, looking for the differences. A clinic, and a lab, and a lunch counter, depending
on the hour.

She did none of it without consent. That was the line. She had crossed it once, in a safehouse with the sun still
up, and she had not stopped thinking about it since.

She was a tall woman herself, nearly six foot three, and tattooed everywhere but her face, all of it flowers. She wore
shirts with pockets so there was always somewhere to keep a pen. She had been a street medic. She did not scare easily.

So when she came round a corner in a quiet part of the ward, late, and found someone bent over a dying patient, she
didn't run and she didn't shout. She looked.

The woman was taller than Claude. Much taller: the far side of seven feet, and built to match, and wrapped in far too
many clothes for the season, with a hat tipped low. She was feeding, and she didn't stop because she'd been seen. She
turned her head, and gave Claude a look that said, as plainly as words, *if you're not quiet, you're next.*

Claude laughed.

The woman tilted her head at that, as if something had just become more interesting. She wiped her mouth. She gave
Claude a little wave, very nearly flirtatious.

"You're going to get caught," said Claude.

"Are you worried for my well-being?"

"Well. No. Not quite. It's against the rules. It's your death."

"It doesn't rise, if that's what you mean. It won't become one of mine." The woman looked down at the patient without
much interest. "I can reduce the corpse to ash. And no one will be any the wiser."

Claude took that in. Someone was going to get killed doing this, she thought. Probably this woman. She had no intention
of being standing next to her when it happened. She was already living on borrowed time.

"Adieu," said the tall woman, touching the brim of her hat, and was gone.

## Informed Consent

Olly had been putting Trieste off.

He'd said he'd come and watch the sun rise with her crew, and he'd meant it, and then there'd been a rave to plan, and
then another thing. He wasn't sure how long it had been. Long enough, probably, that she'd stopped asking and started
assuming. He'd said it out loud. That was how he made himself do things.

He told Claude.

"Please don't," said Claude. "I want to perform science on you. Please don't die." Then she thought about it for a
moment longer. "Or. You could go. And we could test something first. See if there's anything that makes you immune to
the sun."

"You think so?" He was trying very hard to sound like it was nothing. "Look, I'm not scared. I'm going to do it anyway.
But would it make me immune to the sun? That would be cool."

"We could try."

"Have you ever seen that happen?"

"No. But I'm sure there's something we could do."

"Is there a book? Some famous vampire who could go out in the sun?" He shrugged. "I'm not scared. It would just make me
much more likely to go."

So Claude asked Moth for anything on walking in daylight, and on tweaking the blood to do it, and she went looking
online, too, where she found a recipe. It had been posted to a group for people like them, on Reddit of all places, and
in her professional opinion it was absolutely bonkers. It also had ideas in it. She read everything she could find for
anything that might map onto biology: a particular strength of blood, a known Kindred who could do it, any advantage
that could be made rather than born with.

Her setup was more meth lab than witch's cauldron. It always had been. What she couldn't work out, she substituted. She
kept a little supply of blood from as many of the clans as she could get, and on something like a whim she used
Nosferatu, because they were a bit fucked up, and maybe something in that kept them going; and Gangrel. And a
medicine. Sunscreen, in the end: liquefied, the base of it, worked in. She tuned it as close to what she thought would
work as she could get, and called it about eighty percent.

"If you're going to do this," she said, "drink this first. Eight hours before, maybe, to be sure it's in your system.
And then I need you to remember everything that happens to you."

"If I don't die."

"And, here." She had a form. She was not entirely joking. "Sign here. Name, date of birth. You can say no at any point."

"But actually I can't."

"You can."

He liked that, more than he let on. Rami had never once told him what was going into him. Rami had *sold* him, more or less,
for a favour, and Olly had decided not to think too hard about what it meant that someone could do that. But he and Claude
had hit it off. And there was a kind of interest in it for him, too. Whatever was happening to his body, here was someone
who wanted to understand it.

"So for the gate thing," he said, "I'm the vessel. But this one, I just get to drink it?"

"This one you just drink."

"Oh, this is great." He looked at the vial. "Are you going to have some?"

"I—" Claude considered. "Yes. For science."

"Come hang out outside," said Olly. "We'll see how it goes."

## The Rooftop

Trieste's people were a lot of people. Olly had met some of them before; she ran with a crowd, and if they were dying off one
by one at these things, he'd probably met at least one of the ones who wouldn't make it. They were cool, he kept telling himself.
Seriously. Cool.

There was talk, while they waited, the way there is on a roof in the last hour of a night. Somebody said something about a
mass grave, and Olly lost the thread. Where they slept? Or a lot of people had died there recently?

It was neither. They meant where they'd woken up. Not that morning. The first time.

"Oh," said Olly. "Whoa." He'd thought they were being mysterious about something else. They'd been killed, he gathered. All
together. Murdered, and then they'd come back up out of the ground.

Claude was tapping her foot. Olly noticed it before he noticed anything else about her.

He wasn't worried about the sun, exactly. That surprised him. What he couldn't stop thinking about was that he and Claude were
standing on a roof with Trieste's crew, all of them there to take their chances, with something in their systems that gave them
an edge nobody else had. It felt like a party foul. He was almost more socially anxious than scared. *Don't mention the drug*,
he kept telling himself. *Don't mention the drug.* Maybe that was a mechanism. It was working, anyway.

The sky went grey, and then less grey.

He watched Trieste, because he assumed this was the part where you went inside, and she wasn't moving. So he stayed calm, or
looked it.

Claude watched everything. She was a scientist and she meant to use every second of this to make the next batch better, and
she watched the light come, and she watched the others, and she looked down at her own arms.

The sun came up, and it hit them, and it didn't burn.

It *tingled*. That was the closest she could come. Like being burned, without the burning. Olly felt the warmth of it on his face
for the first time since he'd died.

One of Trieste's people did not get that. Somebody went up into ash as the light touched them, and the rest moved fast, and not in
a panic; they'd done this before. They got the ash into a bag and went down, into their bolt-hole in the sewers, and were gone.

Olly and Claude went the other way.

---

"Trieste hangs out with some interesting people," Olly said, once they were somewhere nobody could hear. "They're cool, though.
Seriously." Then: "What did it feel like? For you? Because I could feel the warmth of the sun."

"A little tingly," said Claude. "Like I was being burned, but it didn't happen."

"I don't know what it would have felt like if we hadn't done this. Have you seen the sun? Since?"

"No. You?"

"No." He laughed, a little wildly. "Well. We made it. Maybe we'll see the sun again now."

"I wouldn't trust it to always work," said Claude. "It would take a very consistent regimen. Making it fresh."

"But you did it. You're a *wizard*. I don't know how you pulled this off."

"We could sell this stuff," said Olly.

"That's probably true," said Claude. "How much do you think we'd get for it?"

"Like, a lot." He thought about who'd buy it, and who'd want to know where it came from. "But it has to be someone we can trust.
We can't let the word out to just anybody. Not that Reddit group. Don't share it in the Reddit group. I don't know what the hell
they were thinking."

"Maybe we could compete with Rami."

"Hey," said Olly. "He's the boss."

A beat.

"For now," one of them said.

## A Roof Over Her Head

"I ran into somebody," Claude said, "feeding on a dying patient."

She told him about the tall woman. Very interesting. Very tall. And an idea had come to her on the roof, watching the light: there
might be plenty of this to go around, if they could get it working. They would need people.

So Claude went back to the hospital, and waited, and kept an eye out, and almost on cue the tall woman came in.

"My partner and I have a proposition for you," said Claude. "If you're interested. But we shouldn't hang around here."

"So you wish to make a proposal with me." The woman considered her. "Very well, then. I'll listen." A pause. "I mean to find a good
place with a roof over my head. You have my attention."

Her name was Valti.

She was not forthcoming. She stuck to herself, she said, and people tended to leave her alone, so long as she didn't act suspicious
and didn't draw attention. All the layers were for the sun: they kept her from barbecuing herself in it. She slept in a vacant
apartment building, condemned. Who had made her, she couldn't say. The change had blurred it, the way it blurs a lot of things.

Claude gave her an address, away from the territory they'd met in, and told her to come and talk properly.

## The Tax Office

They needed a place of their own. After the safehouse, and the men in the helmets, Claude wanted two things from wherever they went,
and she wanted them very much.

The first was somewhere to work. Olly was all for it. He was no scientist, but he was entirely in favour of the vampire meth lab thing
that was happening.

The second was a way out. She was not going to walk into a new place with people she barely knew and not know how to leave it. She had
told Olly what happened in the safehouse, in detail, not least the part where she'd killed a man. It might have been two weeks ago. It was
still very fresh.

They found a basement suite under a strip of shops on the Danforth. It had been a tax office. They put the lab in, and they found the second
way out, the one that wouldn't be on anybody's plan.

---

Valti came back, later.

The condemned building wasn't somewhere she could stay anymore. She didn't say why. She didn't sound afraid of anything; she only sounded
like someone who needed somewhere else to sleep.

Claude and Olly asked her to come again, properly, and talk.

---

There were other things settling into place, those weeks. Olly had started carrying for the Pony Express: packages, drives, whatever
needed to cross the city by hand. His sister Isabelle was a public defender, and the only person
he knew who did good on purpose, for a living. Whatever he had in him that wanted to make the comfortable uncomfortable, and be a thorn in the
side of people with power, he'd got from her.

And Claude, who had crossed the one line she had, still meant to do no harm. She had sworn it once, and she held to it the way she held to the
rules that were written down. There was a doctor at a Toronto hospital, Celeste, a friend she had come up through the field with. Claude
thought about Celeste when she thought about what kind of doctor she was still trying to be.
